Case reopened

This file is about the Eureka Stockade at Ballarat, on Wadawurrung Country. Some linked accounts of the battle describe wounds and deaths in detail.

1

Open the envelope

Before dawn on Sunday 3 December 1854, soldiers and police marched from the Government Camp at Ballarat to a stockade built by gold diggers on the Eureka lead.

Captain Thomas led 276 men. Inside the stockade there were far fewer diggers.

Shooting broke out. Within minutes it was over.

Everyone agrees there was shooting. Not everyone agrees who started it.

Every date, place and quotation here is from the records cited below.

What reliable sources have established. See the bibliography ↓

  • During 1854 police checked digger licences, and the diggers grew angry. 12
  • On 29 November a huge meeting on Bakery Hill burned licences and raised the blue Southern Cross flag. 1
  • On 30 November Peter Lalor led the diggers in an oath under the flag. 1
  • Thomas’s force of 276 men (100 mounted, 176 on foot) left the Government Camp early on 3 December; Thomas wrote, “At 3 o’clock I left”. Witnesses differ on the time of the attack: Carboni woke at “full dawn, not daylight”. 31
  • Thirteen men, including Raffaello Carboni, were tried for high treason in 1855. All were found not guilty. 14

Evidence A

Official report · same day

Captain J. W. Thomas’s report to Head Quarters

“were received by rather sharp and well-directed fire from the insurgents, without word or challenge on their part; then, and not till then, I ordered the bugle to sound the ‘commence firing;’”

Source. Captain J. W. Thomas, report to Head Quarters, Camp, Ballaarat, 3 December 1854. Government records held by Public Record Office Victoria; transcription on Wikisource.

Look for. Written the same day, for his superiors. What did he need them to believe?

Three witnesses, three questions

Ask every witness the same three questions. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

Witness 1

Peter Lalor

The diggers’ leader · inside the stockade

“I believe that one or two signal shots were fired by our sentries.” He also wrote: “The military fired the first volley.” A signal shot is a warning; a volley is many guns fired together.

Source. Peter Lalor, “To the Colonists of Victoria”, The Argus, 10 April 1855, p. 7.

Witness 2

Captain John Wellesley Thomas

Commander of the soldiers and police

His men “were received by rather sharp and well-directed fire from the insurgents, without word or challenge on their part”. His report does not mention a warning shot from a sentry.

Source. Captain J. W. Thomas, report to Head Quarters, 3 December 1854.

Witness 3

Raffaello Carboni

An Italian digger · asleep in a tent beside the stockade

His tent was “the second from the stockade, on the hill, west.” “A discharge of musketry—then a round from the bugle—the command ‘forward’.” He does not say which side fired that first discharge.

Source. Raffaello Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (1855), chapter LVI.

5

Decide: my finding

Before you decide: inquiry questions on people, documents, silences, and what is still unsettled

People

Who was there? Who speaks, and who is spoken for?

  • Who was inside the stockade, and who was outside it?
  • The sentries are never named. What would you ask them?

Documents

Who made each record, when, and for whom?

  • A report written the same day, a letter four months later, and a book a year later: who was each written for?

Silences

What is missing, lost, or never asked?

  • What question did no one ask Captain Thomas?
  • Women of the goldfields, such as Anastasia Hayes, appear in later notices (1892), but no source in this case says she saw the first shot.

Still unsettled

What can the record not settle?

  • Who fired first?
  • Nobody knows exactly how many died: diggers, soldiers, and at least one woman.

Who fired first?

The Bias Button · go further

A word can hide an act.

Every source was made by someone, for a reason. Some words soften what happened. Some leave people out. Some tell you who belongs, who to blame, or what to do next. The Bias Button asks the same four questions of every source, from a paper of 1858 to a post from this week.

  1. Who made it, and why?
  2. Who is missing?
  3. What are the loaded words doing?
  4. What does it want you to think or do?

The button does not give the answer. “We can’t know” is a fair finding when a source will not support a firm claim.

Four investigations in the Bias Button app

The first two are about racism in historical sources, the core of the app. The next two use the same button on gender, wartime and class. Each ends with a modern comparison: a real, dated, published example, never an invented one, and never one that names a private person. Some sources use racist, sexist or violent language; the app carries content notes and quotes only short extracts.

1. Hidden words

Some official and newspaper words sound milder than the act they describe.

Sources include The Moreton Bay Courier, 1858; The Sydney Morning Herald, 1912. Trove ↗

2. Who is allowed in?

A law can draw a line without naming a race. A masthead can say the line out loud.

Sources include Federal Hansard extract, 1901; Immigration Restriction Act 1901. Federal Register of Legislation ↗

3. Send a man

Wartime posters and later articles can tell women what they are for.

Sources include Australian War Memorial poster, 1915; The Daily Telegraph, 1938. Australian War Memorial ↗

4. Who gets the label?

Class bias often arrives as a short, sticky word.
