Briefing

In this guided investigation, you step into the role of investigator. Together, the whole room reopens one Australian history case file: Eureka, Ballarat, 1854.

Using primary sources, evidence cards, three witnesses and a Bias Button activity, you will reopen the record in a culturally appropriate and apolitical way. You will examine different perspectives and publicly available records, through specific inquiry questions that explore the people, documents, silences, and questions the official story never fully settled.

By the end of the session you will leave with the Eureka case file, the Bias Button activity, a ready-to-use approach for immediate classroom use, and links to all six case files (Deep Time, Batavia, Eureka, Federation, Freedom Ride and Frontier) for the year levels you teach.

The guided session

Read. Ask. Decide.

Read

Open the envelope and the evidence cards: an official report, a letter to the newspapers, an eyewitness book, the first Melbourne report and a diary.

Ask

Three real witnesses, the same three questions: What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? Then press the Bias Button.

Decide

Write your finding and the one piece of evidence behind it. Say how sure you are. “We can’t know” is a fair finding when the record will not support more.

We can’t know

Sometimes the most honest finding. At Eureka, the people who were there still disagreed about the first shot. Saying what the record cannot tell us is part of the work.

Case reopened

This file is about the Eureka Stockade at Ballarat, on Wadawurrung Country. Some linked accounts of the battle describe wounds and deaths in detail; teachers should preview them first.

1

Open the envelope

Before dawn on Sunday 3 December 1854, soldiers and police marched from the Government Camp at Ballarat to a stockade built by gold diggers on the Eureka lead.

Captain Thomas led 276 men. Inside the stockade there were far fewer diggers.

Shooting broke out. Within minutes it was over.

Everyone agrees there was shooting. Not everyone agrees who started it.

Every date, place and quotation here is from the records cited below.

What the record agrees on.

  • During 1854 police checked digger licences, and the diggers grew angry.
  • On 29 November a huge meeting on Bakery Hill burned licences and raised the blue Southern Cross flag.
  • On 30 November Peter Lalor led the diggers in an oath under the flag.
  • Thomas’s force of 276 men (100 mounted, 176 on foot) left the Government Camp early on 3 December. The exact time is disputed.
  • Thirteen men, including Raffaello Carboni, were tried for high treason in 1855. All were found not guilty.

3

Three witnesses, three questions

Ask every witness the same three questions. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

Witness 1

Peter Lalor

The diggers’ leader · inside the stockade

“I believe that one or two signal shots were fired by our sentries.” He also wrote: “The military fired the first volley.” A signal shot is a warning; a volley is many guns fired together.

Source. Peter Lalor, “To the Colonists of Victoria”, The Argus, 10 April 1855, p. 7.

Witness 2

Captain John Wellesley Thomas

Commander of the soldiers and police

His men “were received by rather sharp and well-directed fire from the insurgents, without word or challenge on their part”. His report does not mention a warning shot from a sentry.

Source. Captain J. W. Thomas, report to Head Quarters, 3 December 1854.

Witness 3

Raffaello Carboni

An Italian digger · asleep in a tent beside the stockade

His tent was “the second from the stockade, on the hill, west.” “A discharge of musketry—then a round from the bugle—the command ‘forward’.” He does not say which side fired that first discharge.

Source. Raffaello Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (1855), chapter LVI.

5

Decide: my finding

Before you decide: inquiry questions on people, documents, silences, and what is still unsettled

People

Who was there? Who speaks, and who is spoken for?

  • Who was inside the stockade, and who was outside it?
  • The sentries are never named. What would you ask them?

Documents

Who made each record, when, and for whom?

  • A report written the same day, a letter four months later, and a book a year later: who was each written for?

Silences

What is missing, lost, or never asked?

  • What question did no one ask Captain Thomas?
  • Women of the goldfields, such as the teacher Anastasia Hayes, appear in later notices (1892), but no source in this case says she saw the first shot.

Still unsettled

What can the record not settle?

  • Who fired first?
  • Nobody knows exactly how many died: diggers, soldiers, and at least one woman.

Who fired first?

There is no answer at the back. Write only what these records can support. “We can’t know” is an honest finding.

The Bias Button activity · take it further

A word can hide an act.

Every source was made by someone, for a reason. Some words soften what happened. Some leave people out. Some tell you who belongs, who to blame, or what to do next. The Bias Button asks the same four questions of every source, from a paper of 1858 to a post from this week.

  1. Who made it, and why?
  2. Who is missing?
  3. What are the loaded words doing?
  4. What does it want you to think or do?

The button does not give the answer. “We can’t know” is a fair finding when a source will not support a firm claim.

Four investigations in the Bias Button app

The first two are about racism in historical sources, the core of the app. The next two use the same button on gender, wartime and class. Each has a modern comparison chosen in class: a real, dated, published example, never an invented one, and never one that names a private person. Some sources use racist, sexist or violent language; the app carries content notes and quotes only short extracts.

1. Hidden words

Some official and newspaper words sound milder than the act they describe.

Sources include The Moreton Bay Courier, 1858; The Sydney Morning Herald, 1912. Trove ↗

2. Who is allowed in?

A law can draw a line without naming a race. A masthead can say the line out loud.

Sources include Federal Hansard extract, 1901; Immigration Restriction Act 1901. Federal Register of Legislation ↗

3. Send a man

Wartime posters and later articles can tell women what they are for.

Sources include Australian War Memorial poster, 1915; The Daily Telegraph, 1938. Australian War Memorial ↗

4. Who gets the label?

Class bias often arrives as a short, sticky word.

Sources include The Murrumbidgee Irrigator, 1931; The Canberra Times, 1976. Trove ↗

Take it to your classroom

A ready-to-use approach

Presenter note. The same structure works across Years 4 to 10. Eureka runs well with the whole room; in class, choose the case for your year level, from Deep Time and Batavia (Year 4) to the Freedom Ride (Year 10). Frontier (the Bathurst War and Myall Creek) is suited to Year 8/9.

The same routine works for every case file and every app in the Reopen the Record family. In a classroom, run it in one long lesson, or split it across two.

5 min

Open the envelope

Read the opener aloud. Ask: what do we think we already know? Write it up, to test later.

10 min

Read the agreed file

Students mark what is agreed and what is still disputed. Teach any new words.

15 min

Evidence stations

Print the evidence cards. Groups rotate, noting who made each source, when, and for whom.

15 min

Ask three witnesses

Same three questions for every witness. Compare answers across groups.

10 min

Bias Button

Each group presses the button on one source and reports one loaded word and one missing voice.

10 min

Decide

Each student writes a finding, one piece of evidence, one thing we can’t know, and sure, maybe or guess.

5 min

Close the file

Return to the opening list. What changed? What would we need to settle the question?

Suggested classroom timings: about 70 minutes in all. For two lessons, stop after the witnesses.

Investigation roles

Lead Investigator

Keeps the case question in front of the team, and keeps time.

Archivist

Tracks every source: who made it, when, where it is held, and the link.

Witness Examiner

Asks each witness the three questions, and asks “how do you know?”

Bias Checker

Runs the Bias Button and flags loaded words and missing voices.

Recorder

Writes the team’s finding and the “We can’t know” list.

Rotate roles between sessions, so every student practises every skill.

Super Sleuths extension

  • Open the full case app and its Super Sleuths tab: timelines, source packs, sure / maybe / guess, and harder comparisons.
  • Find one more real source on Trove, the National Archives, a state library or the Australian War Memorial. Cite it fully. Does it change the finding?
  • Swap witnesses with another team and test their finding against your evidence.
  • Write a “Case reopened” note: evidence for, evidence against, and what we still can’t know.
Teacher Note

What is true on this page. Every date, place and quotation comes from the cited records, as already checked for the live Reopen the Record apps, or from the linked public source. Nothing is invented. Several apps open with a page labelled Imagined (a constructed notebook or diary page, based on real events). Those openers are not reproduced here. Tell students which parts of an app are imagined, and ask how they could check.

Cultural safety. Follow the AIATSIS Code of Ethics and the protocols of the local communities whose Countries and histories each case concerns. Use only openly shareable, public-record content. There is no secret or sacred material, ceremony, retold cultural story, rock art image or photograph of a deceased Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander person on this page; silhouettes are used. Seek local guidance before adding community voices, oral histories or images. Keep the advisory notes on student materials.

Oral histories. In the other case files, recorded interviews are linked to their public catalogue entries (National Library of Australia), and published recollections to their source. Living people are quoted only in their published words.

Harmful language and violence. Racist historical material is linked to the archive or quoted as a short, attributed extract with a content note. Killings are named and counted, not described. Some linked reports, memoirs and diaries describe the fighting at Eureka and its dead: preview them before class.

An apolitical approach. The routine asks what a source can prove, not what to think. Frame modern comparisons as a study of published language, not a verdict on a person.

Year levels (for teachers only). Student pages carry no year level. The levels in the take-away section come from each app’s own Teacher Note. The same structure works across Years 4 to 10. Frontier is suited to Year 8/9; the app’s own note maps it to Australian Curriculum v9 Year 9 History (AC9HH9K03), so check the fit for your class.

Corrections. If something here should be corrected, it will be updated. The note asking for corrections is permanent.
